## 1. Current situation of bus service

### Bus terminal and parking infrastructure

The bus terminal system in Ho Chi Minh City includes dedicated bus
terminals, passenger transfer hubs, intercity bus stations, specialised
parking systems for cars and taxis, and several small parking lots.
These facilities had a total area of 39.16 ha in the fifth period
(2011--2017). The existing station and vehicle parking area is limited,
accounting for less than 0.1% of the urban area. It currently reaches
about 71.62 ha compared with 1,141 ha in the master plan for
construction of Ho Chi Minh City until 2025. Bus station locations are
unsuitable in some cases, and available land is limited. Regarding
infrastructure, the station and parking system serving public passenger
transport by bus includes 27 berths with a total area reported as 18,357
ha, accounting for 22.62% of the area in the Prime Minister's master
plan 568/QD-Tg.

With metro lines still incomplete, buses remain a major form of public
transport, carrying more than 30% of passengers in the city's total
traffic volume (HCMC Department of Transport, 2021). The city has about
130 bus routes, including intra-city and intercity routes (90 subsidised
and 36 non-subsidized routes), two tourist service routes, and a river
bus route (waterbus) (Ho Chi Minh City Public Transport Management
Center, 2021). The bus network reaches 55.3% of the city's district,
commune, ward, and township divisions. Routes form a fan-shaped network
with two main centres: Ben Thanh and Cho Lon (HCMC Department of
Transport, 2022).

### Public passenger transport volume

Between 2016 and 2019, public passenger transport volume increased
slightly, from 567 million passengers per year to 632.5 million.
Policies focused on implementing and improving the service quality of
buses, taxis, and technology-based motorbike taxis. From 2019 to 2021,
however, transport volume declined due to the COVID-19 pandemic.
Transport services were suspended or operated at limited capacity to
maintain distance between passengers. In 2020, volume fell to 385.5
million passengers per year (246 million fewer than in 2019, or 1.64
times lower). In 2021, it fell further to 159.6 million passengers per
year (472.9 million fewer than in 2019, or 3.96 times lower). Fear of
infection in public places also limited public transport use. A survey
of 48 bus users found that more than 50% (26 people) had stopped or
reduced their bus use during the pandemic. Most had switched to private
vehicles for safety, but used them only as temporary alternatives.

## 2. Current situation of the traffic environment in the CBD

The human experience of the traffic environment---including roads and
their surroundings, vehicles, and drivers---is shaped by interactions
with these elements. The driver component is not discussed here because
this section analyses only the physical components. Although the
environment is often viewed as static, it also acts as a dynamic force
shaping how space is used. People are active participants in the traffic
environment, but they are also influenced by their surroundings, which
partly shape their perceptions.

Environmental perception is based mainly on sensory stimuli, including
visual, auditory, olfactory, and tactile sensations. Spatial experience
is therefore closely linked to human behaviour, including mode choice in
urban environments. Given the same traffic-environment inputs, users may
make different assessments because of demographic differences. This
section assesses the environment-based bus-riding experience in Ho Chi
Minh City, including conditions at stops (bus shelters, bus stops,
information signboards, and related facilities) and inside buses.

An interesting feature of Ho Chi Minh City's central business district
(CBD) is the uneven distribution of bus stops. There are 150 stops
across key roads such as Nguyen Hue and Pasteur. This distribution may
relate to local population density: areas with more people, such as
Districts 1, 3, 5, and 10, may have more transit options. The number of
businesses may also matter, since companies need convenient access for
clients and employees. However, this pattern is not universal across the
CBD. Phu Nhuan, Go Vap, and Tan Binh districts are described as less
well served by buses, with fewer transport hubs, while motorbikes and
private vehicles provide alternative commuting options. Areas near
railway and bus stations may also have fewer bus stops because people
can choose other modes of transport.

Bus stops are typically located in residential neighbourhoods and public
places such as schools, parks, hospitals, and administrative buildings.
This distribution is intended to improve access for commuters travelling
to different destinations. The function of the surrounding area and the
available sidewalk space influence stop design. Pillar stops are often
used in residential areas because they take up little space in front of
homes. Bus shelters are more common at public places such as parks,
museums, schools, and shopping malls, where they provide a more
sheltered waiting area.

Within the CBD, the study identifies 150 bus stops and eight information
signboards accessible to public transport passengers. These include 44
uncovered pillar stops, 46 covered pillar stops located with central
shade trees, 37 renovated bus shelters, and 15 bus shelters lacking
important elements such as lighting, cameras, and information boards.

Sidewalk width also influences stop type. Pillar stops are commonly used
on sidewalks less than 1.5 metres wide because they are compact and
occupy less space. Bus shelters are usually found on sidewalks 1.5
metres wide or more, providing a more comfortable and sheltered waiting
area.

Approximately 85%--88% of the land in the city centre is covered by the
bus system (served area of bus/traffic area), but only 10% of the
traffic area is reserved for buses as special bus lanes. The Ho Chi Minh
City Public Transport Management Center has been working to finalise a
bus-priority-lane scheme, although the available area may be
insufficient to improve travel times and traffic flow for riders. The
study describes four possible configurations for special bus lanes: near
the right curb, in the middle of the carriageway, on the left side of
the route, and on the opposite side of the road to the left in the
opposite direction. It also reports operating hours for priority lanes
on Vo Thi Sau and Dien Bien Phu of 6:30--8:30 a.m. and 3:30--5:30 p.m.,
Monday through Friday (Ngoc An, 2019).

The areas around bus stops in HCMC's CBD are often busy, with
pedestrians moving alongside street vendors and motorbikes passing
through traffic. This creates challenging waiting conditions at ordinary
roadside stops without shade or seating. In some areas, motorcycles use
sidewalks during congestion, making it harder for passengers to board
buses. Other stops offer calmer environments, particularly where wide
sidewalks sit beneath leafy trees.

### Equipment at bus stops

**Figure 4. Lighting.**

![Figure 4: Lighting at bus stops](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f4.avif)

Most bus shelters in HCMC's CBD appear to have adequate lighting, with
one or more fixtures providing illumination for passengers travelling at
night. Some locations nevertheless have inadequate lighting because
bulbs have burned out or fixtures are not bright enough.

**Figure 5. CCTV cameras.**

![Figure 5: CCTV cameras at bus shelters](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f5.avif)

Some renovated bus shelters have security cameras mounted on their roofs
and directed toward the station area. These cameras may deter vandalism
and crime and help identify incidents afterward. However, this form of
surveillance is limited and absent from some locations.

**Figure 6. Benches.**

![Figure 6: Benches at bus stops](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f6.avif)

Bench seating is provided at various bus stops across HCMC's CBD,
offering places to rest while waiting for transport. The study reports
benches at 34.6% of stops. Typical benches are made of metal and are
often brightly coloured, but some are worn out or missing.

**Figure 7. Information boards displaying bus schedules.**

![Figure 7: Information boards displaying bus schedules](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f7.avif)

Access to timely and reliable schedule information helps passengers plan
journeys. The study reports that most bus shelters (24.6%) provide a
visible information board with bus schedules and routes. These boards
can support efficient trip planning and sustainable travel, although
some are inactive.

**Figure 8. Bus information boards.**

![Figure 8: Bus information boards](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f8.avif)

Some information boards may contain inconsistencies or inaccuracies, so
passengers should not rely on them as the sole source for trip planning.
The boards provide journey maps, bus routes, station locations, and
estimated travel times between stops. Their design, however, is not
always easy to understand or use.

**Figure 9. Roofs.**

![Figure 9: Roofs at bus stops](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f9.avif)

Some bus stops have damaged roofs or no roof at all, including some
pillar stops and information signboards. Uncovered stops account for
34.6% of all bus stops and are located around the periphery of the CBD.

### Vehicles

route table, boarding door, and exit door (left to right).

![Figure 10: Bus appearance, timetable, transfer information, boarding door, and exit door](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f10.avif)

**Figure 11.** Interior space, stop-request button, emergency hammer,
and camera (left to right).

![Figure 11: Interior space and safety equipment on a bus](assets/images/hcmc-travel-mode-choice/w2_f11.avif)

Buses are equipped with modern passenger-support facilities, including
air conditioning, stop-request buttons, and stop-announcement speakers,
as well as safety equipment such as emergency hammers and fire
extinguishers. A departure timetable and free-transfer route table on
the outside of the vehicle help passengers follow their journey and
choose transfer options. However, the lack of ramps at boarding and
alighting entrances makes it difficult for passengers with disabilities
to enter and exit public buses in HCMC.

## Conclusion

The distribution of bus stops in the CBD and Ho Chi Minh City more
broadly is influenced by population density, business activity, and land
use. Sidewalk width and land use are also considered when choosing stop
types. Although the study reports that bus shelters account for 65.4% of
all bus stops, many shaded stops with trees (pillar stops) are located
at the edge of the CBD, where large cargo vehicles often travel. This
may affect the health, safety, experience, and equity of bus users.

Street vending, as well as motorcycles parking or travelling on
sidewalks during rush hours, negatively affects the environment around
bus stops. The quality and availability of amenities are uneven,
creating unequal experiences for passengers. Many shelters have
information boards showing timetables and routes, but some may not be
updated regularly or may be difficult to use. Buses in the CBD have
modern facilities, yet the absence of ramps at boarding and alighting
entrances creates difficulties for passengers with disabilities.
